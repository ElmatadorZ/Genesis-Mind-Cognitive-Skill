Probably yes, but only if you negotiate the terms. As offered, the deal gives the client most of the upside and leaves you with most of the risk. A guaranteed 40% of revenue is valuable to a 5-person shop. Exclusivity is the costly part, and you should price it and limit it.

## What you're really being asked to sell

You aren't just selling design hours. You're also selling your option to work for anyone else in that industry for a year, plus however long it takes to rebuild that pipeline afterward. That has a price, and it should show up in the fee.

## The core risks

- **Concentration.** Once one client is 40% of revenue, they have leverage over you: scope creep, slow payment, "can you just..." requests. Many agencies treat 25–30% from one client as the danger line.
- **The cliff at month 12.** If they don't renew, you lose 40% of revenue at once, and your pipeline in that industry will have been dead for a year. This risk is bigger than the contract itself.
- **Loss of positioning.** If that industry is your specialty or your main source of referrals, exclusivity hurts you far more than if it's one vertical among many.
- **Capacity lock-in.** If the work takes up 40%+ of your team's time, you may have to hire for it. Then you carry fixed costs that depend on a client who can leave.

## Questions that decide it

1. **What share of your current pipeline and portfolio comes from that industry?** If it's small, exclusivity costs you little. If it's your core niche, the price should be much higher, or you should walk away.
2. **What do they mean by "the industry"?** "Fintech" is very different from "consumer neobanks in our country." Narrow definitions are cheap. Broad ones are expensive.
3. **Can you exit early?** And can they? A 30-day termination right for the client with no matching protection for you is a red flag.
4. **Would you need to hire?** If yes, the downside of not renewing gets much worse.
5. **Are your current clients in that industry?** Existing clients should be excluded from the restriction by name.

## What to negotiate

- **An exclusivity premium.** Usually 15–30% on top of your normal rate, because you're giving up market access.
- **A narrow, written definition** of the competitors you can't work with, ideally a named list rather than an entire industry.
- **Existing clients excluded** from the restriction.
- **No tail on exclusivity,** or a very short one. The restriction ends when the contract ends, not 6–12 months later.
- **Minimum commitment and notice:** a fixed monthly fee whether or not they use the hours, and a 60–90 day termination notice (or a kill fee) so you aren't hit with a cliff mid-year.
- **A defined scope and hour cap,** with overage billed at your rate, so the retainer doesn't grow into 60% of your capacity for 40% of your revenue.
- **Payment terms:** monthly in advance if possible.
- **A renewal decision by month 9,** so you have 3 months to rebuild if they don't renew.

## How to run it if you sign

- Treat the retainer as a foundation, not your whole business. Keep actively selling outside that industry from day one.
- Use the steady cash flow to build reserves: aim for 3–6 months of operating costs by month 12.
- Avoid permanent hires tied to this client. Use freelancers for the extra load where you can.
- Bring them deeper value (strategy, reporting, embedded work) so renewal is likely and on your terms.

## Bottom line

- **Take it** if the industry isn't your core market, exclusivity is narrowly defined and priced in, and you have notice and termination protection.
- **Push back hard or decline** if the industry is your main niche, they want broad exclusivity at your normal rate, or they can walk out with little notice while you stay restricted.
