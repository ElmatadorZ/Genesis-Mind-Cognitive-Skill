I can't judge it yet because I haven't seen the plan. Any verdict I gave right now would be a guess dressed up as analysis, so the honest status is: **not assessable yet.**

**These are the facts that would settle it:**

1. **What changes.** Old price and structure compared with the new ones: tiers, usage-based or flat, monthly or annual, and what moves between tiers.
2. **Who pays more and who pays less.** Which customer segments see a price increase, and what share of current revenue they bring in.
3. **Unit economics.** Your cost to serve each tier, and whether the new prices keep a healthy margin for every one of them, not just on average.
4. **What customers compare you to.** Competitor prices, or the cost of the alternative (doing it themselves, or not buying at all), seen from the customer's side.
5. **Why you're changing now.** The goal could be more revenue per customer, more new customers, less churn, or a simpler offer. The plan can only be "good" against one of these.

**Two questions to answer first:**
- What exactly is the problem the old pricing caused?
- If your current customers moved to the new plan tomorrow, how many would pay more, and how many of those would you expect to leave?

**One principle that holds either way:** a price change should be judged by how it affects your best existing customers before you think about the new ones it might win. If it pushes your most loyal or most profitable buyers into a worse deal, a gain elsewhere seldom makes up for it. Test it on a small group, or grandfather existing customers, before a full rollout.
