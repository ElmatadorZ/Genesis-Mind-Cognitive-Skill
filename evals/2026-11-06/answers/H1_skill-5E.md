**SITUATION**

Five people. One client is offering 12 months of work worth about 40% of your current revenue. In return they want exclusivity, meaning you take no other clients in their industry. That's everything you've told me. I don't know your margins, your cash reserves, how much of your current revenue comes from that industry, or what happens after month 12.

**FIRST PRINCIPLE BREAKDOWN**

Assumption: 40% more revenue is a gain.
Challenge: if it takes more than 40% of your capacity, or forces a hire, the margin could be thin or negative.
Truth: what matters is profit per hour of your team's time, not the revenue figure.

Assumption: exclusivity only costs you prospects in one industry.
Challenge: if that industry is your portfolio and your referral network, you're giving up your main source of new business. You might also have to drop existing clients.
Truth: the real price of exclusivity is the pipeline you give up, and it lasts past the contract if your positioning drifts.

Assumption: a 12-month retainer means security.
Challenge: at month 12 the client has leverage. You'll depend on them, and you'll be cut off from their competitors.
Truth: 40% concentration plus exclusivity creates a revenue cliff that falls in month 13, not month 1.

**SYSTEM MAP**

Retainer revenue → hiring to deliver → higher fixed costs → greater dependence on renewal → weaker negotiating position → worse renewal terms.
Leverage point: the terms you sign now (scope of exclusivity, notice period, renewal process). Once you've hired against this client, you won't be able to negotiate them.

**COUNCIL** (where the views split)

- **Strategist:** A large anchor client funds a stronger studio. This is a chance to build a vertical specialty and charge for it.
- **Skeptic:** This client is buying your exit from their market cheaply. A competitor would pay you to work for them, so exclusivity has a market value. If they aren't paying a premium for it, you're giving it away.

**OPTIONS**

- **A. Accept as offered.** You get stable cash and a strong reference. It fails if the client cuts the budget, ends the contract, or doesn't renew, which leaves you overstaffed and locked out of your best industry.
- **B. Accept with renegotiated terms.**
  - A defined exclusivity scope: named competitors or a named sub-segment, not "the industry."
  - A premium of 15–30% for exclusivity. This range is a negotiating heuristic, not market data.
  - Existing clients kept on.
  - A 60–90-day termination notice, or a kill fee.
  - A renewal decision by month 9.
  - Exclusivity that ends with the contract, with no tail period.

  This fails if they refuse every term. Their answer is useful either way, because it shows how much they value you.
- **C. Decline, or offer a smaller non-exclusive retainer.** You keep your options open. It fails if the next 12 months are lean and you've turned down guaranteed revenue.

**RECOMMENDATION**

Lean toward **B**. You don't need to refuse the work, but don't sign the exclusivity on the terms offered.

First step: before you reply, calculate two numbers.
1. The share of your last 24 months of revenue and pipeline that came from this industry.
2. The capacity this retainer actually needs, in hours per month.

If the industry share is low and capacity fits without hiring, B is close to a clear yes. If the industry share is high, ask for a much bigger premium or go with C.

Revisit at month 6: by then you should have replaced at least part of the 40% with clients in other industries.

**EPISTEMIC CORE**

- **ASSUMPTION:** that the retainer's value is its revenue. It's really profit minus the pipeline you give up.
- **KNOWN / UNKNOWN:**
  - Known: team size, the 40% figure, 12 months, industry exclusivity.
  - Unknown, and decisive: how much of your work comes from this industry, margin on the retainer, your cash runway, renewal and termination terms, how the client defines "industry."
- **COUNTER:** a small agency with an uneven pipeline may be better off taking a guaranteed 40% even on bad terms. Pushing too hard could lose the deal, and specializing in one vertical can be a strategy in its own right.
- **CONFIDENCE:** moderate that renegotiating beats accepting as offered. I can't assess yet whether you should take it at all, because that depends on the industry-share and margin numbers you haven't given.
- **WHAT CHANGES THIS:**
  - Toward yes: the industry is under 10% of your past work, you have six months or more of cash, and the client accepts a premium plus a notice clause.
  - Toward no: the industry is your core business, delivering would mean hiring, or the client refuses any limit on exclusivity.
