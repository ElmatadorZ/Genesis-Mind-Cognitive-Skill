The most important number in your question is 14 months. Start there, because runway changes what "expansion" really costs you.

## 1. Work out how much time you actually have

14 months of runway is not 14 months to experiment. Plan on roughly:

- **Fundraising takes 4–6 months.** You want to start raising with at least 6 months of cash still in the bank, or you'll be negotiating from weakness.
- **So you have about 8 months** to produce the results your next round will be judged on.
- **A new country usually takes 6–12 months to show real signal.** That covers entity setup, hiring, localization, payments, regulatory work, and finding the go-to-market approach that works there.

Put those together and Vietnam's results would probably still be unclear when you need to raise. You'd be pitching a half-finished expansion and a Thailand business that slowed down because your attention was split. That's the worst outcome, and it's the default risk here.

## 2. The question underneath: how strong is Thailand?

Whether to expand mostly depends on whether you've really won your first market. Be honest about these:

- **Retention and unit economics:** Do customers stay? Is the cost to acquire a customer paid back within a reasonable time (under 12 months)? Is that getting better or worse?
- **Headroom:** Have you reached a meaningful share of the market you can serve in Thailand, or are you still at single digits? Is growth slowing because the market is saturated, or because of things you can fix, like product, channels or sales?
- **Repeatability:** Could a new hire run your acquisition approach from a written playbook, or does it still depend on the founders' relationships?

**If Thailand is still growing well and you're far from saturation**, doubling down almost always wins. Investors would rather see one market dominated than two markets half-built. Growing faster in a market you know is cheaper and lower risk.

**If Thailand is clearly hitting a ceiling**, because the market is small, growth is flattening and acquisition costs are rising, then expansion becomes a real need. Vietnam may be the right move.

## 3. What Vietnam would really cost

Don't just count the direct costs. Include the hidden ones:

- **Direct costs:** legal entity, local hires (a country lead, sales or ops), localization, marketing to launch, and possibly regulatory or licensing work, depending on your sector.
- **Founder attention:** This is usually the biggest cost. Someone senior has to own Vietnam, and that person stops working on Thailand.
- **How different the markets are:** Vietnam has a larger, younger population (~100M) and is often more price-competitive. It has its own language, payment habits, distribution channels and competitors. A playbook that works in Thailand may only partly carry over.
- **Effect on runway:** Estimate how many months of runway Vietnam would use up. If the answer is "3–4 months of our 14", ask whether the expected payoff justifies that before your raise.

## 4. A middle path: test cheaply, commit later

You don't have to choose all or nothing. A sensible plan for your situation:

1. **Next 6–8 months: double down on Thailand** and aim for metrics you could raise on, such as growth rate, retention and payback.
2. **At the same time, run low-cost Vietnam tests,** using maybe 5–10% of spend:
   - Interviews with potential customers or partners
   - A localized landing page or small paid campaign to measure demand
   - Serving a few Vietnamese customers remotely, if your product allows it
   - Mapping regulation, competitors and partners
3. **Set clear go/no-go criteria in advance,** for example: "If we get X sign-ups or Y pilot customers at a CAC below Z, Vietnam becomes the main use of our next round."
4. **Make Vietnam part of the fundraising story:** "We've won Thailand, and here's the evidence Vietnam is our next market." That's often a stronger pitch than "we launched Vietnam and it's early."

## 5. When expanding now might make sense

Some situations justify an earlier move:

- A large customer or partner will pay you to enter Vietnam, so the expansion is funded and de-risked.
- There's a real first-mover window, for example a competitor is about to lock up Vietnam or a regulatory change opens a door.
- Investors have explicitly said a multi-country presence is what they need to fund your next round.
- Thailand is clearly saturated and has stopped growing.

If none of these apply, the default answer is **double down on Thailand, gather Vietnam evidence cheaply, and expand with your next round's money, not this round's.**
